Classed maps and symbolsSlides 42–45

A graduated color map (choropleth) shades each polygon by class. A graduated symbol map puts a sized marker on each feature. Points take marker symbols, lines take line symbols and polygons take shaded fills. Interval and ratio data must be divided into classes before mapping, then shown by size, thickness or color.

Rule of thumb: totals (people, dollars) suit symbols, while rates, densities and scores suit a choropleth. In ArcGIS Pro: Symbology pane → Primary symbology → Graduated Colors, Graduated Symbols or Proportional Symbols.

Classifying data and layer symbologySlides 46 and 48–49

Same data, different classifications. The choice changes how the map looks and whether it tells the truth, and the best method depends on how the data are distributed and what the map is for.

ArcGIS Pro: right-click the layer → Symbology, then set Field, Normalization, Method, Classes and Color scheme. The histogram view lets you drag breaks, which switches the method to Manual Interval. The 2022 ArcMap slides use Layer Properties → Symbology → Quantities (slide 49).

Group exercise: the risk index by countySlides 50–52

Using the shapefiles you have (Georgia state-maintained routes and the county risk index), make a formal map and tell a short story with it. Read the data dictionary first and choose symbology that fits the field.

Watch out: the shapefile already carries FEMA's own national ratings (risk_ratng): 86 Georgia counties are “Very Low”, 58 “Relatively Low” and 15 “Relatively Moderate”. Those are national classes. The classes on this page are drawn from Georgia's 159 values only.

Pitfalls students hit

  • Mapping a raw total such as population or expected annual loss as a choropleth. Big counties look important because they are big. Normalize, or use symbols.
  • Using Quantile on skewed data. It forces equal class counts, so small differences in the middle get different colors while very different counties at the top share one.
  • Using Equal Interval on skewed data. A few extreme counties stretch the range, and most of the state ends up in one or two classes.
  • Comparing two maps (two years, two hazards) that each have their own Natural Breaks. Jenks breaks belong to one data set. Use the same manual breaks on both maps.
  • Keeping the default legend labels. Round the breaks and the labels, and state the units.
Data: FEMA National Risk Index county data, from the lab's GA_Counties.shp (159 counties; boundaries simplified to about 250 m, 40 m in the District 7 counties). Field names follow the shapefile (risk_score, eal_valt, sovi_score, resl_score, population, area, buildvalue, trnd_risks, rfld_risks, hwav_risks); scores use the scale of the NRI release in the lab files. Neighboring states: U.S. Census Bureau cartographic boundaries (us-atlas). Projection: d3.geoConicConformal with standard parallels 31°25′N and 34°17′N and central meridian 83°30′W, on a sphere, which mimics Georgia Statewide Lambert. Natural Breaks uses the exact Fisher–Jenks optimal solution, so it should match ArcGIS Pro's Natural Breaks closely. ArcGIS does not publish its Geometric Interval algorithm; this page searches for the ratio between class widths that makes the class counts most even, as the ArcGIS documentation describes, so its breaks can differ slightly from Pro's. Standard Deviation uses the population standard deviation (divide by n) and groups everything beyond ±2.5 SD (±2 SD for ½ SD intervals) into an open-ended class at each end.
