Datum Lab

A latitude and a longitude name a place only after you say which datum they belong to. A datum is an ellipsoid of a chosen size, placed so that it fits the Earth's real shape. Change the datum and the same numbers land somewhere else, about 17 m away at Georgia Tech, which is enough to move a crash off the road it happened on.

    Define Projection vs Project

    Pick a mistake and see where the data lands. Define Projection only writes the label (the .prj file) and never moves a coordinate. Project computes new coordinates in another system. ArcGIS Pro's on-the-fly projection draws layers with different but correct .prj files on top of each other, so a layer that does not line up is almost always mislabelled.

    Try this in class

    1. In Shape of the Earth, drag the flattening slider down to ×1. The ellipse becomes a circle to the eye, because a − b is only 21.4 km, 0.34 % of the radius. Drag it back to ×100 to see why the lecture still calls the Earth an ellipsoid.
    2. Pick Clarke 1866 and tick “All three ellipsoids”. Even at ×100 the three outlines sit on top of each other. Ask the class why NAD 27 and NAD 83 still disagree by 17 m in Atlanta, then answer it with Fit a datum.
    3. In Fit a datum, press the local preset and read the three RMS gaps, then press the geocentric preset. Then drag the ellipse yourself and try to beat both. You cannot, because the local preset is already the best possible fit for North America, and the geocentric preset is the best possible fit for the whole Earth.
    4. Slide the latitude from 0° to 80° and watch 1° of longitude shrink from 111 km toward zero while 1° of latitude barely changes. At Atlanta a buffer “of 0.001°” would reach 111 m north and south but only 93 m east and west, so on the ground it is an oval.
    5. In Same numbers, different place, compare Savannah with Brasstown Bald. Count lane widths on the arrow and decide whether the crash would still sit on the same road.
    6. Step through the four mistakes in the playground. For each one, say out loud which tool fixes it: Define Projection, Project, or redoing Display XY Data.

    Pitfalls students hit

    • Define Projection is not Project. Define Projection only writes the .prj label and never moves coordinates; use it only to correct a missing or wrong label. Project computes new coordinates in another system. Projecting data that was labelled wrong bakes the error into the coordinates.
    • Display XY Data needs the right GCS. The crash CSV is in GCS WGS 1984, so pick that coordinate system in the dialog (XY Table To Point in ArcGIS Pro). Put Longitude in the X field and Latitude in the Y field.
    • Check the datum transformation. When layers sit on different datums, ArcGIS Pro applies a geographic transformation, listed under Map Properties > Transformation, and Project has a Geographic Transformation box. NAD_1983_To_WGS_1984_1 is a zero shift that treats the two as identical; for NAD 27 data pick a NADCON or other NAD 1927 transformation on purpose.
    • Undocumented data. If a file has no .prj and you must guess, the lecture's advice is to try NAD 1983 first (slide 8), then check it against a layer you trust.
    • Degrees are not distances. Never buffer or measure in a GCS. Project to a coordinate system in feet or metres first, such as State Plane Georgia West for the Atlanta curves.

    Back to Lecture 2: from the Earth's shape to a datum

    Slides 2 to 8 build a datum in steps: angles on a round Earth, an ellipsoid, the geoid, fitting one to the other, and the datums North America actually uses.

    Geographic coordinate systemSlide 2

    A GCS locates places with angles in degrees on a three-dimensional surface. Latitude is the angle up from the equator; on an ellipsoid it is measured along the normal, the line perpendicular to the horizon. Longitude is the angle east or west of the Prime Meridian.

    Georgia: at 33.75°N one degree of longitude is about 92.7 km and one of latitude about 110.9 km, so a degree is not a fixed distance.

    Spheroid (ellipsoid)Slide 3

    The Earth bulges at the equator. An ellipsoid is set by its semi-major axis a (equatorial radius) and semi-minor axis b (polar radius). For GRS 80 and WGS 84, a = 6,378,137 m and b = 6,356,752.314 m.

    Why exaggerate: a − b is 21.4 km, so at true scale the ellipsoid looks like a circle.

    GeoidSlide 4

    The geoid is the surface of equal gravity potential that matches mean sea level. It is lumpy because mass inside the Earth is uneven. The NASA picture on the slide exaggerates the lumps, and so does this page.

    Heights: GPS heights are ellipsoidal (h); survey and map elevations are orthometric (H, above the geoid); h = H + N.

    Datum: local vs geocentricSlides 5–6

    A datum shifts an ellipsoid so it fits the geoid. A local datum fits one region best and fits elsewhere worse (best for North America, worse for South America). A geocentric datum puts the ellipsoid's center at the Earth's center of mass and fits the whole Earth.

    Real numbers: the NAD 27 ellipsoid sits about 238 m from the Earth's center of mass.

    The same flagpole, two datumsSlide 7

    The slide's flagpole has one position on the ground but two pairs of coordinates, one in GCS NAD 1983 and one in GCS NAD 1927. Read one pair in the other datum and you are pointing at a different spot.

    Georgia: 17.4 m at Tech Tower, 26.1 m in Savannah, 15.9 m on Brasstown Bald.

    Datums used in North AmericaSlide 8

    NAD 27 uses the Clarke 1866 spheroid and is still found in old data sets. NAD 83 uses GRS 80 and is the usual datum for mapping; NAD 83 HARN refines it with a high-accuracy network. WGS 84 is geocentric and is what most GPS units report.

    Lab: crashes are WGS 1984, curves and roads are NAD 1983, counties are WGS 1984 Web Mercator.

    A datum answers “where is the ellipsoid, and how big is it”. A projection, the next part of Lecture 2, answers “how do we flatten it onto paper”. Every projected coordinate system in the lab (State Plane, Georgia Lambert, Web Mercator, UTM) sits on top of a datum, which is why the .prj of a projected layer still names NAD 1983 or WGS 1984.
